## Email 2: The Biggest Lie About Career Fear

**Send:** Day 2

**Subject line options:**
1. The lie that kept me in medicine too long
2. "You should be grateful" — let's talk about that
3. Gratitude is not a cage

**Preview text:** Someone said this to me six months before I quit. It almost worked.

---

When I told a colleague I was thinking about leaving medicine, she looked at me like I'd said something obscene.

"You should be grateful. Do you know how many people would kill for your position?"

I went home that night and couldn't sleep.

Not because she was wrong. Because she was partially right.

I was grateful. I had worked incredibly hard to become a doctor. I respected the profession. I cared about patients.

And I was also miserable. Depleted. Losing myself one 96-hour shift at a time.

Here's the lie: that gratitude and dissatisfaction can't coexist.

They can.

You can be thankful for what a career gave you and still recognize it's taking more than you have left. You can honor what you built and still walk away from it.

Gratitude is not a lease agreement. It doesn't mean you owe your entire life to the thing you're grateful for.

I think about this every morning at the crosswalk. The kids don't analyze whether they deserve to cross. They don't stand on the curb listing reasons they should stay put. They see the sign. They walk.

If you did the Fear Audit, look at what you wrote down. How many of those fears are actually just guilt wearing a mask?

Being scared is honest. Guilt disguised as gratitude is a trap.

You can be grateful and still leave.

Sahawat

## Email 3: One Crosswalk Lesson

**Send:** Day 4

**Subject line options:**
1. The morning a Toyota Camry taught me about authority
2. The cars always stop
3. What a stop sign taught me that medical school never did

**Preview text:** My first morning as a crossing guard, I was sure a car wouldn't stop.

---

My first morning holding the stop sign, I was terrified.

Not the kind of terrified I felt in the ER. Different. Quieter.

I stepped off the curb, raised the sign, and watched a Toyota Camry coming down the street.

My whole body tightened.

What if it doesn't stop?

What if the driver doesn't see me? What if they don't care? What if I'm not enough — not important enough, not authoritative enough — to make a two-ton vehicle stop?

The Camry stopped.

Not because of my medical degree. Not because of my resume or my credentials or the years I spent in training.

Because I showed up and held up the sign.

That's it.

I think about this constantly. How much of my life I spent believing I needed more — more qualifications, more approval, more proof that I was allowed to take up space.

The crosswalk doesn't care about your CV. It cares that you showed up.

You have more authority than you think. Not the kind that comes from a title or a corner office. The kind that comes from deciding you're going to stand in the road and hold up your hand.

If you're stuck in a career you're afraid to leave, consider this.

You don't need permission to take up space in your own life.

You just need to step off the curb.

Sahawat

## Email 4: What Actually Happened When I Left

**Send:** Day 7

**Subject line options:**
1. What I lost when I left medicine (and what I found)
2. I won't pretend it was easy
3. The honest version of my "career change" story

**Preview text:** I lost the title, the salary, and the respect of people I loved. Here's the rest.

---

I want to tell you what actually happened when I left medicine. Not the cleaned-up version. The real one.

What I lost:

The title. Doctor. Gone. The word that had defined me since I was twenty years old.

The salary. I went from a physician's income to a crossing guard's wage. That math is not subtle.

Certainty. I had a path. It was brutal, but it was a path. Suddenly I had nothing mapped.

Respect from some people. Not everyone. But enough. Enough that it hurt.

My uncle stopped calling. A friend told me I was wasting my potential. Someone I respected said I was "throwing it all away."

That was real. I won't pretend it wasn't.

Now here's the other side.

I sleep. Not the broken, anxious four hours between shifts. Actual sleep.

I am present. When someone talks to me, I'm there. I'm not mentally triaging the next twelve hours.

I have peace. Not happiness every second — peace. The kind where you lie down at night and your chest isn't tight.

And I have a new identity. Not one I inherited or performed or earned through suffering. One that actually fits.

I'm a crossing guard who helps people cross — literally and, now, in a different way.

I'm not going to tell you it's easy to leave. It wasn't for me. The first six months were lonely and strange and full of doubt.

But I'll tell you this: I have never once wished I was back in that hospital at 3 a.m.

Not once.

If the Fear Audit stirred something in you, sit with that. Don't rush it. But don't bury it either.

Sahawat
