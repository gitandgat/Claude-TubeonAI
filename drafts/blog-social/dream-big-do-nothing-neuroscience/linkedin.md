Your brain built an avoidance loop — and it's been rewarding itself for years.

When you think about the hard thing — leaving, pivoting, taking the first step — your amygdala fires a threat signal. Dread shows up. You close the tab. You feel relief. Your brain logs that relief as a reward.

That reward is the trap.

Researcher Tim Pychyl found that procrastination isn't a time-management problem. It's an emotion regulation problem. People aren't avoiding the task — they're avoiding how they expect the task will make them feel.

And they're almost always wrong.

His research: when people were finally forced to begin, they nearly all said the same two things: the task wasn't as bad as they feared, and they wished they'd started sooner.

Every avoided step strengthens the avoidance pathway. Every avoided step weakens the action pathway. The longer the loop runs, the more automatic it becomes — until not moving feels like just how things are.

The fix isn't more motivation. It's an interruption.

Name the emotion. Shrink the action to something your amygdala won't flag as a threat. Start for five minutes. The dread before the crosswalk is always worse than crossing it.
