You don't have a motivation problem. You have an amygdala problem.

When you imagine leaving — a different role, a different life — and then do nothing, your brain isn't being lazy. It's doing exactly what it was designed to do: protect you from perceived threat.

Researcher Tim Pychyl spent years studying procrastination and arrived at an uncomfortable finding: it's not a time-management failure. It's an emotion regulation failure.

The sequence:
→ You think about the hard thing
→ Negative emotion spikes (anxiety, overwhelm, fear of failure)
→ You avoid it
→ You feel relief
→ Your brain logs that relief as a reward
→ Repeat

Over years, that loop doesn't just persist. It deepens. The avoidance pathway gets more automatic. The action pathway atrophies.

For healthcare professionals, this loop is everywhere. The dream of a different path shows up — and immediately behind it comes dread. "What if I lose everything? What if I'm not qualified for anything else? What if I fail publicly?" The amygdala fires. You close the browser tab. You feel better. Until next time.

Here's what the neuroscience actually says:

You're usually not avoiding the task. You're avoiding how you expect the task will make you feel. And your brain is often wrong about how bad it will be.

Pychyl's research found that when people were finally forced to begin, almost all of them said the same two things:
— the task was not as bad as they expected
— they wished they had started earlier

The fix is deceptively small.

Not motivation. Not a career coach. Not waiting until fear is gone.

Just start. Five minutes. Sized small enough that your amygdala doesn't even register it as a threat.

Name the emotion. Shrink the action. Begin.

The dread before the crosswalk is always worse than crossing it.
