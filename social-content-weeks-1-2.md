### LinkedIn Post 1 — Pillar: Fear of Leaving
**Topic: "The sunk cost trap kept me in medicine for 2 extra years"**

I stayed in medicine two years longer than I should have.

Not because I loved it.

Because I had already given too much to leave.

Six years of medical school.
Residency.
The sleepless rotations.
The money. The time. The youth I spent studying while everyone else was living.

I kept telling myself:
"You've come too far to quit now."

That sentence almost killed me.

Because here is what nobody explains about sunk cost:

The investment becomes the cage.

Every year you stay, the cage gets smaller.
Every year you stay, the cost of leaving gets "higher."
Every year you stay, you convince yourself it would be wasteful to walk away.

But you are not a bank account.
Your life is not a ledger.
And time you already spent cannot be recovered by spending more of it in the same place.

I lost two years to that lie.

Two years of 96-hour shifts.
Two years of being screamed at by attendings.
Two years of falling asleep in hospital stairwells because there was nowhere else to go.

The day I finally left, I did not feel like I was throwing anything away.

I felt like I had finally stopped throwing myself away.

If you are staying in something because of what you have already invested — not because of where it is actually taking you — I built a free exercise for that.

It is called the Fear Audit.
Five minutes. Five questions.
It will not tell you what to do.
But it will show you what is actually keeping you stuck.

### LinkedIn Post 2 — Pillar: Fear of Leaving
**Topic: "What will people think?"**

When I told my mother I was leaving medicine, she cried.

Not because she was sad.
Because she was scared for me.

In Thailand, being a doctor is not just a job.
It is a family achievement.
It is proof that the sacrifice worked.
It is the thing your parents say at dinner with relatives.

"My son is a doctor."

Four words that carry the weight of generations.

When I left, those four words disappeared.

And what replaced them was silence.

My friends did not know what to say.
Some stopped calling.
Some gave me the look — the one that says "I would never do what you are doing."

I had a colleague tell me, quietly, in a hallway:
"You are making a mistake."

He looked exhausted when he said it.
I think he was talking to himself.

Here is what I have learned about "what will people think":

They will think about it for five minutes.
Then they will go back to their own problems.

But the life you are not living?
That stays with you every single day.

I chose to disappoint some people so I could stop disappointing myself.

That is not selfish.
That is survival.

If the fear of judgment is the thing standing between you and a decision you already know you need to make — I built a five-minute exercise called the Fear Audit.

It helps you separate real risk from social pressure.
Because those are not the same thing.

### LinkedIn Post 3 — Pillar: Permission to Choose
**Topic: "Quitting is not failure. It's a decision."**

I need to tell you something that took me years to understand.

Quitting is not the opposite of success.

Staying in something that is destroying you is not strength.
It is just endurance with no finish line.

We have been taught that quitting means you were not tough enough.
That winners never quit.
That if you just push a little harder, a little longer, it will all be worth it.

I pushed for years.

I pushed through 96-hour shifts where I could not remember my own name.
I pushed through supervisors who threw instruments in the operating room.
I pushed through a system that treated sleep as a luxury and suffering as a rite of passage.

And when I finally stopped pushing, people called it quitting.

I call it the single hardest decision I have ever made.

Because walking away from something you spent your entire life building requires more courage than staying ever did.

Staying was autopilot.
Leaving was a choice.

And choice — real, terrifying, deliberate choice — is not weakness.

It is the first honest thing I did in years.

If you are sitting in something right now, telling yourself that leaving would mean you failed — I want you to try something.

The Fear Audit.
Five minutes.
It will help you see the difference between commitment and captivity.

### LinkedIn Post 4 — Pillar: Stuck Professional's Playbook
**Topic: "How to know if you should leave — the Fear Audit"**

I am a former doctor who is now a crossing guard.

Before I left medicine, I spent months paralyzed.

Not because I did not know I needed to leave.
I knew.

I was paralyzed because I could not separate the real fears from the noise.

Financial risk? Real.
"What if I am making the biggest mistake of my life?" Noise.
Losing my medical license timeline? Real.
"But I worked so hard to get here." Noise.

The problem is that when you are inside the thing, everything sounds equally loud.

So I built something I wish I had back then.

I call it the Fear Audit.

It is a five-minute exercise. Here is how part of it works:

Step one: Write down every fear you have about leaving. All of them. The rational ones. The ridiculous ones. The ones you have never said out loud.

Step two: Next to each fear, write one of two labels.
"Fact" — meaning this is a concrete, measurable consequence you can plan for.
"Story" — meaning this is a narrative you are telling yourself based on assumption, guilt, or social pressure.

Most people find that 70 to 80 percent of their fears are stories.

That does not make them less real in your body.
But it does make them less useful as decision-making tools.

The full Fear Audit has five steps.
It will not tell you to quit your job.
It will not tell you to stay.

It will help you see clearly.
And clarity is the thing most stuck professionals are actually missing.
